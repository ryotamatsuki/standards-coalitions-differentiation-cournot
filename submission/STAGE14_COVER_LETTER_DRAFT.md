Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, **“Endogenous Product Design and Participation in International Compatibility Agreements,”** as an Original Article for the *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.

The paper examines how firms' post-policy product-design responses alter national incentives to enter international compatibility agreements. In a three-country model, we hold governments' bilateral-first proposal and accession procedure, the economic primitives, and the national-welfare objective fixed while comparing privately reoptimized product designs with designs frozen at the no-agreement Nash equilibrium. Firms' redesign affects domestic consumer surplus and resident firms' worldwide profits through rivals' design decisions, and these effects can reverse both a bilateral member's standalone welfare gain and an outsider's accession incentive.

A computer-assisted proof establishes, at a benchmark and in a nonempty open neighborhood, that the implemented outcome changes from a government-optimal integrated agreement under frozen designs to fragmentation under endogenous designs throughout the positive-depth menu of the specified inherited-depth procedure. At the same parameters, a direct three-country agreement can nevertheless improve all countries' welfare relative to fragmentation. The comparison speaks to public-economic questions of externalities, government consent and the policy evaluation of compatibility cooperation, while recognizing that the result is protocol-specific rather than a general impossibility of multilateral cooperation.

We believe the paper's controlled design-response comparison and the distinction between accession and direct-grand rejection alternatives are relevant to JPET's interest in public economics, regulation, voting and international collective choice.

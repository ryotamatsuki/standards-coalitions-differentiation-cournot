\title{International Standards Coalitions and Endogenous Product Design}
\author{}

\begin{abstract}
Why can an international technical standard that benefits every country fail
to emerge? We study a three-government sequential consent game in which two
countries first agree on a compatibility depth, a third may request accession
at the inherited depth, and firms subsequently select costly nonstandardized
product attributes before Cournot competition. At an exact rational parameter
vector, allowing firms to reoptimize these attributes makes every strictly
positive bilateral agreement unattractive to its members and multilateral
accession unattractive to the outsider. Yet a hypothetical grand agreement
at a positive depth increases the welfare of all three countries. Holding
private designs fixed at their no-agreement Nash levels instead leads to an
accepted international agreement under the same governmental sequence.
We certify the continuum of policy-depth inequalities with rational interval
computations and provide the economic decomposition that links firms'
induced design responses to national consumer surplus and exporters'
profits. An independently checked nonlinear design mapping preserves the
existential contrast at a different depth, while a preregistered same-depth
test fails. The result is a model-specific diagnosis of a forecast error
under one bargaining agenda, not a general prediction of compatibility failure.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} International standards; Compatibility;
Endogenous product differentiation; Coalition formation; Network effects.

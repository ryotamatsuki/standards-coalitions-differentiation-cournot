\section{Relation to Existing Theory}\label{sec:literature}

Our result is deliberately narrower than the general claim
that compatibility generates network benefits but reduces
competitive differentiation. Those opposing forces and the
endogenous formation of technical standards coalitions are
well established in analyses of network goods
\citep{EconomidesFlyer1998,EconomidesSkrzypacz2003}.
Our concern is a distinct sequencing question: how
\emph{governmental} compatibility commitments alter the
\emph{subsequent} attributes selected by private firms
and, through the induced national welfare continuations,
change the feasibility of enlarging an initially
bilateral alliance.

The closest governmental standards predecessor is
\citet{GandalShy2001}. In their three-country setting,
government recognition and discriminatory regional
standards unions affect consumer welfare and home-firm
income. Their firms compete in prices with fixed
Salop differentiation; they do not select costly
post-policy residual attributes. Moreover, the absence
of a formal regional union in their recognition game
can coexist with extensive \emph{mutual recognition},
which is not the incompatible standards outcome
labelled $\SW$ here. Their results already establish
the strategic significance of outsiders, exclusionary
union incentives, and standards network benefits;
none of those ingredients alone is claimed as new.

\citet{CabralSalant2014} show that standardization and
technology development need not reinforce one another:
a common standard can weaken incentives for
future innovation. The accessible March 2009
working-paper version has its own proposition
numbering and differs in potentially material
presentation details from the published paper;
we therefore do not assert an unverified one-to-one
mapping of working-paper theorem numbers to the
journal article. Their result already establishes
the \emph{general} standards-versus-private-investment
tension. In contrast, our firms' choices are costly
\emph{residual product similarity} after a
government compatibility commitment, in a
three-country quantity-competition setting. The
research question here is not whether a common
standard necessarily improves innovation, but
whether a specific sequential national-consent
procedure can block an \emph{ex post Pareto-gaining}
international standard.

\citet{BuchholzHattori2024} examine a coalition
formation paradox in which a country's technology
decision and subsequent public-good cooperation
interact. Their technology decision precedes coalition
formation and the relevant payoff concerns private
provision of a public good. Their results warn against
presenting a technology--coalition paradox as a
new category of economic mechanism. We isolate a
different ordering and payoff channel, in which
firms choose nonstandardized product attributes
\emph{after} governments have committed to
technical compatibility, and governments evaluate
domestic surplus plus home exporters' world profits.

The regional versus multilateral mutual-recognition
analysis of \citet{TakaradaKawabata2025} is also
especially relevant because member/outsider welfare
and the degree of product differentiation matter for
standardization incentives. Its standards policy
concerns regulatory mutual recognition and negative
consumption spillovers, rather than the positive
technical-network benefit and private post-policy
design choice in \eqref{eq:utility}--\eqref{eq:pi}.
The presence of an outsider obstacle, or the
effect of \emph{exogenous} product differentiation
on coalition welfare, is already prior art.

Finally, backward induction in a country-coalition
extensive form is an application of established
coalition-formation reasoning
\citep{RayVohra1999}; Rosen's
concave-game result supplies the design Nash
uniqueness tool. Neither is a new proof method
introduced by this paper. We claim only the
\emph{certified conjunction}: under the specified
government procedure, downstream residual
design makes every positive bilateral depth
unacceptable, although a hypothetical international
allocation remains beneficial to every country,
and a controlled frozen-design forecast
predicts a feasible positive international agreement.
Because some closest journal-version proofs remain
unavailable for direct comparison, this delineation
is a source-scoped research claim, not a
claim to have exhausted all possible predecessor
theorems.

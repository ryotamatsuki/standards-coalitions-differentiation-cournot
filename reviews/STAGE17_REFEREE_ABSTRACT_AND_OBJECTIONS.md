# Stage17 referee-facing proposal — NOT a submitted abstract

## Provisional title
**Residual Product-Design Frictions and the Implementability of International Standards Agreements**

## Proposed 193-word abstract (use only after priority clearance)
Can firms' ability to redesign product attributes after a standards commitment change the kind of international agreement that sovereign governments actually prefer to implement? We study a three-country Cournot model in which governments negotiate technical compatibility and firms subsequently choose the similarity of attributes not covered by that standard. Extending the original game by a single resource cost of departing from an installed product design, we distinguish technical-standard compliance from residual product adaptation. We show that the marginal consent conditions for a bilateral agreement and third-country accession reverse at a unique local adjustment-cost threshold under regularity conditions. At a certified parameter configuration, a finite-depth three-country agreement is unanimously acceptable throughout a nondegenerate interval of adjustment costs. More strongly, a global analytical/interval bound shows that the proposer's payoff from this accepted multilateral continuation exceeds its payoff from every possible terminal bilateral agreement, regardless of compatibility depth. Conditional on attainment of a government-stage optimum under the specified inherited-depth accession procedure, its terminal agreement therefore must be multilateral. The result concerns institutional implementability, not the global impossibility of cooperation: direct multilateral offers can bypass the same obstacle in the flexible-design baseline. The exact optimal agreement depth and priority against closely related trade-regulation theories remain open.

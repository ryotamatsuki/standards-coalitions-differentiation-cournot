# English contribution disclosure (provisional)

## Novelty

The research proposes a narrowly identified extension of a three-country model of standards accession and endogenous residual product similarity. Prior work has established network externalities, government standards noncoordination, costly technological compliance, endogenous investment, and differences between sequential and multilateral coalition formation. The proposed additional primitive is neither a new bargaining stage nor a generic fixed cost of complying with the standard. Instead, firms must pay a resource cost when moving their nonstandardized product attributes away from a pre-existing residual design. This permits an internally controlled comparison between highly flexible downstream product redesign and technologically sticky product architecture, while holding the governments' bilateral-first, inherited-depth negotiation procedure unchanged. At a certified parameter point, a strictly monotone private-design response produces a unique local threshold in adjustment-cost curvature at which both the bilateral members' marginal consent and the excluded country's accession incentive reverse. This is a new proposition *within the extended model*. Whether it is new relative to the complete existing literature remains unresolved. In particular, the results of Barrett and Yang on redesign costs, Cabral and Salant on dynamic innovation incentives, and Lambertini and Rossini on endogenous quality and government noncoordination require proposition-level distinction. Consequently, the extension should not yet be advertised as a publication-level priority claim.

## Economic importance

The proposed mechanism separates two notions of technological flexibility. Low costs of adopting or implementing a technical standard may promote compatibility, but low costs of adjusting the remaining product characteristics can have the opposite effect. In the certified original Cournot environment, firms find it privately attractive to make their residual attributes more similar following a standardization commitment. The resulting changes in consumer surplus and domestic exporters' income are not included in any single firm's private design first-order condition. These spillovers can overturn the initially positive national welfare effect of a bilateral compatibility edge and the outsider's incentive to accede to the resulting coalition. The extension predicts that imposing stronger real adjustment frictions on residual design can reduce the adverse private feedback and restore unanimity in the same government game. This does not mean that rigidity is generally socially desirable. It means that lower private adjustment costs need not monotonically expand the set of implementable international cooperation agreements. The result matters precisely because the incentive reversal is expressed at the government participation margin, rather than merely as a comparative static of firm profits or equilibrium quantities.

## Generality

The result exploits a regular interior private-design Nash equilibrium and strict diagonal dominance of its pseudo-gradient Jacobian at the original symmetric fragmentation point. The adjustment-cost coefficient enters the diagonal of this Jacobian with a negative sign, without shifting the baseline design because the penalty is centered on the existing fragmentation equilibrium. In the certified parameter configuration, all relevant policy forcing coefficients and national-incidence loss weights are positive. The Nash policy-response vector is therefore a positive inverse-M-matrix product that declines strictly as adjustment curvature increases. The government member gain is a positive direct term minus a declining induced loss, yielding precisely one local crossing. Exact rational interval arithmetic encloses the initial design root and brackets the crossing between 0.93538 and 0.93539. Strict local participation margins imply positive-depth agreements for sufficiently small technical-standard depth above this crossing. The proof is local to the fragmentation edge and to one validated parameter point, though the strict inequalities suggest extension to a neighborhood after appropriate verification. It does not establish uniqueness of a global institutional transition as all agreement depths are optimized, nor robustness across arbitrary consumer demand functions, country asymmetry or competition modes.

## Limits

The extension remains conditional on an intentionally specified sequence of bilateral agreement and third-country accession, with inherited compatibility depth and no transfers or direct multilateral offers. At the unmodified baseline, direct three-country proposals can be unanimously accepted, so neither the new theorem nor the older nonformation result establishes global impossibility or coalition-proof fragmentation. The original nonformation certificate is complete only at its certified center and a nonexplicit neighborhood, and the new rational verification covers derivatives near zero agreement depth; it does not establish a full global policy taxonomy for every adjustment-cost value. The pre-existing design used as the adjustment-cost benchmark is a controlled modeling reference, not an observed or independently microfounded vintage-specific characteristic. Costs of technical-standard compliance must be distinguished carefully from costs of redesigning residual attributes, especially in interpreting the earlier switching-cost literature. Full published versions of the closest prior articles are not all proposition-audited, notably Cheng and Huang. Thus the responsible next step is a focused novelty test and independent mathematical review, followed by an assessment of whether the mechanism is strong enough for a full research article.

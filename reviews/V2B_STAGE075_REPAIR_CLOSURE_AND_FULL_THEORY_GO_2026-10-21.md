## B. Economic contribution independent of mathematics (~175-word editor-facing pitch)

Technical interoperability standards can improve consumer network use while enhancing competition among firms. Governments sometimes begin with a limited bilateral technical agreement and later invite other participants, rather than choosing the multilateral standard simultaneously. Existing standards theory already explains network benefits, bargaining holdouts and reduced innovation incentives. What may be underestimated is the endogenous response of firms' **nonstandardized product attributes** after a public compatibility commitment. We study three symmetric national governments that first attempt a bilateral standard and consider admitting a third, followed by firms selecting costly residual differentiation before multinational Cournot competition. Two different defensible substitution maps, holding government institutions and economic parameters fixed, exhibit a sharp error from freezing private designs: frozen designs predict a positive-depth multilateral agreement, whereas private reoptimization makes every positive-depth intermediate bilateral agreement unattractive and gives the third country a better outsider option. Strikingly, a compatible grand allocation remains strictly better for **every** country than total incompatibility even after firms have reoptimized. Hence the loss is the failure of a sequential participation institution, not proof that technical interconnection destroys social surplus. This is a scoped institutional diagnostic, not a universal standards policy recommendation.

## C. Strongest skeptical-editor refusal (~140 words)

The core result remains an existential counterexample built from strongly symmetric countries, identical segmented markets and a stipulated bilateral-first accession protocol. The condition preventing bilateral formation is strict but narrow in the candidate witness. The international-entry advantage in the nonlinear frozen-design variant is approximately 9.1×10⁻⁷ at an extremely small compatibility depth g=.005, suggesting that the regime switch may rely on fine payoff cancellation. The experiment also failed at its pre-registered same-g=.05 counterfactual, so robustness must be described as preservation of an **existential** outcome with different admissible depths, not quantitative invariance. Generic bargaining models already establish Pareto-improving coalitions that cannot form, while Cabral–Salant already shows standards/innovation incentives can reverse firm preferences. The paper might therefore still be a special parameterized instance of known institutional holdout, rather than an influential general theory. There is no confirmed empirical case for the precise three-country approval game, and no alternative network microfoundation or institution has been fully certified. A field editor might prefer a shorter counterexample or require a transparent sign-condition theorem.
